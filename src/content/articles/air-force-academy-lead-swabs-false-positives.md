I started it because stories like this one keep ending the same way: the people living with the exposure are the last to find out, and usually from someone with a reason to minimize it. Families in Air Force Academy base housing, managed by Hunt Military Communities, say at least four children in three households have detectable blood lead. Children’s Hospital Colorado described the levels as low and not requiring treatment. Parents had used at-home swab kits on their plumbing fixtures and gotten positives; one mother says every kitchen fixture in her house turned the swab red. Parents say the 10th Medical Group, the base clinic, declined to order blood lead tests after Hunt characterized the swab results as false positives. The Academy told the paper no lead contamination has been found on base, and that one resident’s elevated test was followed by a non-elevated one. Hunt didn’t answer the paper’s questions. At least one home had its fixtures replaced this week.

Let me separate the pieces, because each one is a different failure and they stack.


## What a swab is and isn’t


A lead swab is a chemical indicator. You rub it on a surface, and if there’s lead at the surface it turns red or pink. It’s fast, it’s cheap, it’s a screening tool, and EPA recognizes certain swabs for specific uses. What it doesn’t do is tell you how much lead, whether the lead is leaching into water, or whether anyone’s been exposed. It also can give false positives on some surfaces and false negatives on others. Everyone who sells or uses them knows this.

So “false positive” is a legitimate thing to say about a swab. It is not a legitimate thing to conclude without a confirmatory test. A positive swab is a reason to escalate: a certified lab analysis of the fixture, a first-draw water sample, a dust wipe. Colorado’s health department says exactly that. The right response to “every fixture turned red” is “let’s find out,” not “those don’t count.”


## Who gets to call a result false


Here’s the part that should bother anyone, regardless of what the lab eventually shows. The party that declared the swabs false positives is the housing contractor. The party with the financial interest in the fixtures being fine. And on the strength of that declaration, a medical provider declined to order the one test that would have settled the question for the children: a blood lead level, which is a routine, cheap, standard pediatric lab.

A blood lead test is not a water test. It doesn’t depend on whether the swab was right. It measures the child. If a parent has any reason to suspect exposure, a positive swab is more than enough, the test is indicated, and the landlord’s opinion of the swab is irrelevant to the order. That some of these kids now have detectable blood lead, confirmed elsewhere, is the answer the clinic could have had weeks earlier.


## The chain of custody point


This is why lab-analyzed, chain-of-custody sampling exists. A swab in a parent’s hand can be dismissed by anyone with an interest in dismissing it. A fixture sample or a water sample collected per method, sealed, logged, and analyzed by an accredited lab, with a report that states the concentration and the method, can’t be waved away as a false positive. It can be argued with, but on the data, not on the credibility of the person who held the swab.

For any parent in any housing, military or not: a positive swab is step one. Step two is a lab, not a landlord. And a pediatrician who won’t order a blood lead test because a property manager said the swab was wrong is a pediatrician to ask again, in writing.

## What we know

- At least four children in three Air Force Academy base housing households have detectable blood lead, described by Children’s Hospital Colorado as low and not requiring treatment (The Gazette, October 8, 2026).
- At-home swab kits returned positives on plumbing fixtures in several homes. Parents say the 10th Medical Group declined to order blood lead labs after housing contractor Hunt Military Communities characterized the swabs as false positives.
- The Academy says no lead contamination was found on base and one elevated resident test was followed by a non-elevated result; Hunt did not respond. At least one home’s fixtures were replaced this week.

## What we don’t know yet

- Lab results on the fixtures or water.
- The measured blood lead levels.
