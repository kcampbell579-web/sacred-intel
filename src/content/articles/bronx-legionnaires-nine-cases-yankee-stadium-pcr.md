The Melrose and Morrisania cluster I wrote about Monday is now nine confirmed cases and one death. City investigators ran preliminary PCR testing and found Legionella at ten cooling towers, one of them at Yankee Stadium. All ten were ordered drained, cleaned, and disinfected by Monday.

Two things to understand. The first is what PCR does and doesn’t tell you. The second is why a building with a clean compliance record can still be the source.


## PCR versus culture

PCR, polymerase chain reaction, looks for Legionella DNA. It’s fast, it’s sensitive, and it comes back in hours. It’s the right tool when you need to know today which towers to shut down.

What it can’t do is tell you whether the organism is alive. DNA from dead bacteria, killed by last month’s biocide dose, lights up a PCR test the same as DNA from a thriving colony. It also can’t tell you the strain. There are dozens of Legionella species and serogroups, and only some cause disease, and only one of them is the one that infected these nine people.

Culture, growing the organism on a plate, answers both questions. It takes seven to ten days, sometimes longer. If a tower grows live Legionella pneumophila serogroup 1, and the isolate matches the patients’ isolates, that tower is the source. Until then, ten towers are suspects and none is convicted. Culture results are expected within about two weeks of September 13.

So the order to drain and disinfect all ten is the right call under uncertainty. It’s also possible, even likely, that most of them had nothing to do with it.


## Why compliance and control are different things

New York requires cooling tower registration, a maintenance plan, and testing on a set schedule. The Bronx cluster emerged in a neighborhood where, per Healthbeat’s earlier reporting, 25 of 27 registered towers had submitted samples. So the system was largely working on paper.

Here’s the gap. A quarterly test is a snapshot. Legionella in a tower can go from undetectable to dangerous in a few weeks of warm weather, low biocide, and a stagnant dead leg in the piping. A tower can pass in June and seed a cluster in August. The test tells you the tower was fine on the day of the test. It doesn’t control the tower on the other 89 days.

Control is the maintenance plan actually running: biocide dosed on schedule, residual checked, the basin cleaned, the drift eliminator intact, the dead legs flushed. That’s the ASHRAE 188 water management program I keep mentioning. The testing verifies the program. It doesn’t replace it. A building that treats the quarterly sample as the whole obligation has a compliance record and no control, and this is what that looks like.

One more thing about Yankee Stadium. A positive PCR on the most famous tower in the borough is going to get more attention than the other nine combined, and it may turn out to be a false lead: a large, well-funded facility with a professional water treatment contract is not the profile of a tower that seeds an outbreak. The profile is the mid-size building with a tower nobody’s looked at since spring. Don’t let the headline pick the suspect for you. Wait for the culture.

For anyone tracking cooling tower obligations in a compliance platform: the testing interval is the easy field. The maintenance log is the one that matters.
