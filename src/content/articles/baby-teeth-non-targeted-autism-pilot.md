Researchers at UT Health San Antonio ran donated baby teeth from 22 children with autism and 16 typically developing children through non-targeted two-dimensional gas chromatography with time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Published in Neurotoxicology and Teratology.

Both groups averaged about 300 chemicals per tooth. Of 60 compounds found in more than half the samples, 39 are known developmental toxicants or endocrine disruptors. Fifty-seven of those 60 were higher in the autism group, 19 significantly so. Five of the nine with the biggest differences can leach from plastic bottles.

The authors say, clearly, that this is a small pilot and does not establish causation. I’m going to say it louder, and then explain why the paper matters anyway.


## Why a tooth


Primary teeth form in utero and during the first years of life, laying down layers like tree rings. Chemicals in the mother’s and then the child’s blood get incorporated into those layers as they form. When the tooth falls out at six or seven, it carries a record of exposure from before birth through early childhood, time-stamped by layer.

That’s a retrospective biomonitoring matrix. Blood and urine tell you what’s in a person now. A tooth tells you what was in them during the developmental window that neurodevelopmental research cares about, years after the fact, from a sample the family was going to put under a pillow. For exposure science, that’s close to the holy grail, and the method for reading it is what this paper is really demonstrating.


## Why non-targeted


A targeted panel looks for the chemicals you named. Non-targeted analysis, which I wrote about with the 215-pesticide study, records everything the instrument sees and matches it against a library. Three hundred chemicals per tooth is what that produces: not thirty you expected, but the actual inventory. The plasticizers, the fragrance compounds, the flame retardants are things nobody would have put on a targeted list for a tooth study because nobody knew to.


## How to read a 38-child pilot


Thirty-eight kids. Twenty-two in one group, sixteen in the other. That’s enough to generate hypotheses and nowhere near enough to test them. With 60 compounds compared across two small groups, some will differ by chance; 19 “significant” differences out of 60 comparisons needs a correction for multiple testing, and the paper should say whether one was applied. The groups may differ in ways that have nothing to do with chemicals: where they lived, what they ate, what the family could afford. And autism has a strong genetic component; a chemical association, even a real one, is one factor among many.

So: the finding is “these chemicals were higher in this small autism group.” It is not “these chemicals cause autism.” Anyone who writes the second sentence from this paper is writing fiction.


## What it’s actually for


It’s a proof of concept that the tooth-plus-non-targeted method works, that it can detect developmental toxicants, and that it can find group differences worth following up with a study ten times the size. That’s a real contribution. The next study will have hundreds of kids, pre-registered hypotheses, and corrections for multiple testing, and it’ll either confirm a handful of these compounds or it won’t.

For a parent reading the headline: the method is new, the sample is tiny, the cause is not established, and the one thing in the paper that’s actionable, that some of the compounds come from plastic bottles, is advice you’ve already heard. Glass and stainless are fine.

## What we know

- UT Health San Antonio pilot (Neurotoxicology and Teratology; release October 5, 2026): non-targeted GCxGC-TOFMS on primary teeth from 22 children with autism and 16 typically developing children.
- ~300 chemicals per participant in both groups; of 60 compounds in >50% of samples, 39 are known developmental toxicants or endocrine disruptors; 57 of 60 higher in the autism group, 19 significantly; 5 of the 9 largest differences can leach from plastic bottles.
- Authors state the pilot does not establish causation.

## What we don’t know yet

- Whether multiple-comparison corrections were applied.
- Whether the findings replicate in a larger cohort.
