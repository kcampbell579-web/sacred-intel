OSHA’s Philadelphia office cited a Franklin County, Pennsylvania excavation employer on October 7 with willful and serious violations after a young worker died in a trench collapse. The finding: no cave-in protection. Penalties proposed. A willful citation means OSHA concluded the employer knew the requirement and chose not to meet it.

The day before, at a job site on Texas 62 south of I-10 in Orange County, Texas, a 39-year-old worker was buried when the sides of a previously excavated trench collapsed. Coworkers dug him out before emergency crews arrived. He died at a hospital in Beaumont. No employer named yet; the investigation is open.

Two deaths, two days. I write about trench collapses because they’re the most reliably preventable fatality in construction and they keep happening at the same rate. The rule is simple. The dirt doesn’t care that you know it.


## Why trenches kill


A cubic yard of soil weighs about 3,000 pounds. A trench wall that lets go drops several yards onto whoever’s below. The person is pinned, chest compressed, unable to breathe, and the weight is far too much for coworkers to dig out by hand in the few minutes before the brain runs out of oxygen. People who are buried to the chest sometimes survive; people buried past the head almost never do. The Texas crew got their man out, and it still wasn’t fast enough. It never is.

OSHA’s standard, 1926 Subpart P, is built around one requirement: any trench five feet or deeper gets a protective system. Sloping the walls back to a safe angle for the soil type. Benching. A trench box or shield. Shoring. Past 20 feet, a registered engineer designs it. And a competent person, someone trained to classify soil and spot hazards, with the authority to stop work, inspects the trench daily and after anything changes.

Franklin County had none of that. Willful.


## The re-entry trap


The Texas case is the one I want every foreman to read twice. “Previously excavated.” The trench was opened earlier, probably the day before. The walls stood. The crew came back and went in.

Here’s what happens to an open trench overnight. The soil dries at the face and loses cohesion. Or it rains and it saturates. Vibration from traffic on Texas 62 works through it. The tension cracks that form parallel to the edge, which the crew may not have noticed, widen. A wall that was stable at quitting time is a different wall at 7 a.m., and it looks exactly the same.

The standard’s answer is the daily inspection: the competent person looks at the trench before anyone enters, every shift, and after every rain, every vibration event, every change. If the inspection happened in Texas, it missed the cracks. More likely it didn’t happen, because “we were just in there yesterday” is the most common last sentence in this category of fatality.


## The field guide


For anyone who opens ground:

Five feet, a protective system. No exceptions, no “just for a minute.” The minute is when it goes.

A competent person inspects before every entry. Before the shift, after rain, after a nearby truck, after lunch if the sun’s been on it. Written. A trench that was fine yesterday has no standing today.

Look for tension cracks on the surface parallel to the trench edge. They’re the wall telling you it’s about to leave.

Spoil pile two feet back from the edge. The pile’s weight is on the wall.

Ladder within 25 feet of every worker, in any trench four feet or deeper. The exit has to exist before the emergency does.

Nobody goes in to rescue. The second victim in a trench collapse is the coworker who jumped in after the first. Call it in, get equipment, don’t dig by hand. This is the hardest rule and the most important one.

If the box is on the truck and not in the hole, the hole is closed. The box doesn’t work from the truck.

Pair these two in your next toolbox talk. The Pennsylvania one is the employer who didn’t bother. The Texas one is the crew that thought it was fine. Both are dead the same way.

## What we know

- OSHA cited a Franklin County, PA excavation employer with willful and serious violations after a young worker died in a trench collapse; employer failed to provide cave-in protection (OSHA, October 7, 2026).
- A 39-year-old worker died after a previously excavated trench collapsed on Texas 62, Orange County, TX, ~3:38 p.m. October 6, 2026. Coworkers extracted him before emergency crews arrived. Employer not yet named; investigation open.
