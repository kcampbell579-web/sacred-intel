I wrote about atrazine two weeks ago: a quarter of U.S. river and stream miles above EPA’s 9.7 parts per billion threshold for harm to aquatic life, 70 million pounds applied a year, banned in more than 60 countries. EPA has a mitigation plan. A Center for Biological Diversity analysis in the Journal of Environmental Quality now puts a number on what the plan would accomplish.

About 120 (123, to be exact) of more than 11,000 watersheds would come below the threshold. Roughly one percent.

The plan cuts maximum annual application rates for several crops by 20 percent. But, as Notre Dame’s Jason Rohr put it in describing the study, more than 90 percent of the relevant acreage can be exempted or can comply largely through practices already in use. A 20 percent cut that applies to under 10 percent of the acres is a cut of a couple of percent in total load, and the watersheds are over the threshold by a lot more than that.


## How to read a mitigation plan


This is the transferable lesson, because mitigation plans are everywhere: pesticide registrations, stormwater permits, air permits, remediation work plans. They all have the same shape: a list of actions, a projected outcome, and a gap between them that only shows up when someone does the arithmetic.

The questions that find the gap. What fraction of the sources does the action actually apply to, after exemptions? What’s the baseline, so a “20 percent reduction” has a denominator? What’s the measured outcome, and who measures it? If the plan doesn’t state the expected concentration at the receptor after the actions, it’s a list of activities, not a mitigation.

A client who hands you a compliance plan as proof of safety has handed you a document that might be the first kind or the second. Running the arithmetic is the job.

For private well owners in farm country: atrazine’s drinking water limit is 3 ppb, surface water sources are most affected, and it’s a standard add-on to a pesticide panel. The mitigation plan isn’t going to change what’s in the well.

## What we know

- CBD analysis (Journal of Environmental Quality; The New Lede, October 6, 2026): EPA’s atrazine mitigation plan would bring ~120 of 11,000+ U.S. watersheds below the agency’s 9.7 ppb aquatic harm threshold.
- The plan cuts maximum application rates for four crops (field corn, sweet corn, sorghum, sugarcane) by 20%; >90% of relevant acreage can be exempted or comply through existing practices (Jason Rohr, Notre Dame, characterizing the study).
- ~70 million pounds applied annually (EPA). ~25% of river and stream miles affected (separate CBD report, September 2026).
- Update: CBD, CEH, and PAN sued EPA over the plan October 14.

## What we don’t know yet

- Whether EPA revises the plan in response.
