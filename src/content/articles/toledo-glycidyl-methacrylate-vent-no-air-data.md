Tuesday night, a 5,500-gallon tank of glycidyl methacrylate at MC Tank Transport on Progress Avenue in north Toledo began releasing. EPA called it a transfer error. Containment failed. Officials made the call to let the reaction vent to completion rather than put crews into it. A one-mile evacuation zone shrank to a half mile Wednesday evening and a quarter mile Thursday. A three-mile shelter-in-place was lifted Wednesday. Residents reported dizziness, burning eyes, and a strong odor.

Glycidyl methacrylate is a reactive monomer used to make resins and coatings. It’s an irritant to skin, eyes, and airways at low concentrations, which is what the residents are describing. It’s also a carcinogen with longer exposure. And when it gets hot or contaminated, it polymerizes, which is the runaway reaction they’re letting burn itself out.


## Vent-to-completion is a real strategy


I want to be fair to the responders, because the decision they made is one hazmat commanders make for a reason. A reactive monomer in a failed tank is a tank that could rupture. Sending a crew in to try to stop a polymerization that’s already running puts those people in the worst possible place when it goes. Letting it finish, from a distance, with the neighborhood evacuated, can be the choice that kills nobody.

That’s an exposure-assessment judgment: the acute risk to crews versus the chronic, lower-level exposure to a neighborhood over 36 hours. It’s exactly the kind of call industrial hygiene exists to inform. And it’s being made, as far as anyone outside the command post can tell, without a public exposure dataset.


## What’s missing


The guidance to the public has been qualitative. “Irritant.” “Stay inside.” “Strong odor.” Not one concentration has been published. Not a fenceline reading, not a mobile monitor result, not a perimeter number that would let someone a mile away decide whether to open a window.

EPA has the equipment. Every Region has an emergency response team with real-time monitors that read volatiles in parts per million, and they’re almost certainly running them. The numbers exist. They’re not being shared, and the result is a neighborhood making decisions about kids and pets and whether to go back based on how it smells.

Odor is not a dose. Some chemicals you can smell at levels far below harm; some you can’t smell at levels well above it. Glycidyl methacrylate has a sharp odor, which is why people are reporting it, and that’s a bad instrument for anyone trying to figure out their own risk.


## What a community is owed during a vent


Here’s what I’d ask for on day one, as a consultant, and what a neighborhood should ask for as residents.

Perimeter readings, published at intervals, with the instrument and the location. Downwind readings, the same. The occupational limit and the acute community guideline for the compound, so the published numbers mean something. A statement of when and how the zone boundaries were set: on modeled plume, on measured concentration, or on distance. And a plan for post-event sampling, surfaces and indoor air in homes nearest the site, before people are told to go back.

None of that is unreasonable. Most of it is being collected. The gap is between the command post and the public, and 36 hours is long enough to close it.

## What we know

- A 5,500-gallon tank of glycidyl methacrylate at MC Tank Transport Inc., Progress Avenue, Toledo, began releasing Tuesday night, October 20, 2026, after a transfer error per EPA. Containment failed; officials are allowing the reaction to vent to completion.
- Evacuation zone reduced from 1 mile to 0.5 mile (Wednesday evening) to 0.25 mile (Thursday). 3-mile shelter-in-place lifted Wednesday.
- Residents reported dizziness, burning eyes, and strong odor.
- No air monitoring concentrations had been published as of October 22.

## What we don’t know yet

- Any measured air concentration, at the perimeter or downwind.
- Expected duration of the vent.
- Post-event sampling plan for nearby homes.
