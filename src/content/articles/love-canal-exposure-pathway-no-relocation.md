The McKiernan family lives on 99th Street in Niagara Falls, about 100 yards north of Love Canal, with industrial contamination in their backyard. Earlier this month, residents and local advocacy groups filed an administrative petition with DEC and the state Health Department. The state’s answer, this week: no evidence toxic waste is escaping the Love Canal containment system, and no public health basis for relocating the family.

DOH said, in effect, that it has not identified a way for the contaminants at the property to reach the people living there. DEC said samples from the 99th Street property don’t so far appear connected to Love Canal, and further testing and cleanup will follow once the homeowners approve.

I’m not going to tell you the state is right or wrong about this yard. I haven’t seen the data. What I can do is explain the sentence DOH used, because it’s the sentence every residential contamination case turns on, and almost nobody outside the field understands it.


## Contamination is not exposure


Here’s the framework. For a contaminant to hurt someone, five things have to be true in sequence.

There’s a source: something released the chemical. There’s a medium: the chemical is in soil, water, air, or dust. There’s a transport mechanism: the chemical can move from where it is to where a person is. There’s an exposure point: a place the person actually contacts the medium. And there’s an exposure route: the chemical gets into the body, by breathing, swallowing, or skin contact.

All five links, and you have a completed exposure pathway. Break any one, and you don’t. Contamination two feet down in a backyard, under grass, that nobody digs in, with no vapors coming off it and no well drawing from it, is a source and a medium with no transport, no exposure point, and no route. It’s contamination. It’s not exposure. “Hasn’t identified a way for contaminants to reach residents” means exactly that: the agency looked for the links and didn’t find them connected.

That’s the finding that lets the state say “contaminated” and “no health basis to relocate” in the same breath. Both are statements about the data. They’re not a contradiction.


## Why homeowners can’t accept it, and why they’re not wrong to push


Because it sounds like being told the poison in your yard doesn’t count. And because the framework has a weakness that residents feel in their bones: it only works if the agency actually looked at every link.

Did they sample at the depth a kid digs? Did they check soil gas under the house, where volatiles would come up through the slab? Did they account for the vegetable garden, the dog that digs, the basement that floods? A pathway analysis that stops at “no well, no vapor” when nobody checked for vapor is a conclusion, not a finding. And a family that’s watched Love Canal for forty years has reasons not to take the first answer.

So the right question for the McKiernans and the groups backing them isn’t “how can you say it’s safe.” It’s “show me the pathway analysis. Which links did you test, with what samples, and which did you assume.” That’s a question DEC and DOH can answer, and the answer either holds up or it doesn’t.


## The other half of the state’s position


DEC’s second statement is the interesting one: the 99th Street samples don’t appear connected to Love Canal. That’s a separate claim, about source, not pathway. Niagara Falls was a chemical manufacturing city for a century. Contamination in a yard a hundred yards from the most famous dump in America might have come from the dump, or from the fill the house was built on, or from a dozen other places. Chemical fingerprinting, the same idea as the PFAS eagle study I wrote about in September, is how you tell. If DEC has that data, it should publish it, because “it’s not from Love Canal” is a claim that requires a fingerprint.


## For anyone in a similar spot


If an agency tells you contamination is present but there’s no exposure: ask for the pathway analysis in writing, link by link. If an agency tells you it’s not from the site next door: ask what the source attribution is based on. Both are legitimate findings when they’re backed by data, and both are easy to assert without it. The framework is sound. The question is always whether someone actually ran it.

## What we know

- NYS DEC and DOH responded to an October 2026 administrative petition from residents and local advocacy groups regarding the McKiernan home on 99th Street, ~100 yards from Love Canal.
- DOH: no evidence of a leak from the containment system; has not identified an exposure pathway from the property to residents; no health basis for relocation.
- DEC: 99th Street samples do not so far appear connected to Love Canal; further testing and cleanup to follow with homeowner approval.

## What we don’t know yet

- The sampling data, depths, and analytes behind both agency statements.
- Whether soil gas or indoor air was evaluated.
