_Disclosure: I run a company that sells lab-analyzed home testing kits, including for PFAS. I started it because stories like this one keep ending the same way: the people living with the exposure are the last to find out, and usually from someone with a reason to minimize it. I write about these stories for the same reason. Weigh what I say knowing that._

I’ve written about the Cove Hollow Road plume three times since September. This is the one where the timeline finally gets laid out in a public meeting, and the timeline is the story.

May 2023: the East Hampton battery energy storage facility catches fire. Roughly 5,000 pounds of Novec 1230 fire suppressant goes on it, along with about two million gallons of sprinkler water. The mixture goes into the ground.

Last winter, around day 1,000: PFPrA, an ultra-short-chain PFAS, is detected in the Bridgehampton Road wellfield about 2,600 feet away, along with PFOS, both well above drinking water limits. Wells come offline.

October 2026: Suffolk County tells residents its investigation is “mostly complete.” The state Health Department confirms there are no plans for an area-wide health study. The resident who lives nearest the highest-concentration test well asks again for biomonitoring, blood tests for the people who drank the water, and the town declines again.

DEC’s written update of October 14, which is the document to cite rather than any of the press coverage including mine, concludes that Novec 1230 by itself is not a persistent PFAS risk, but breaks down into ultra-short-chain PFAS when it mixes with water under fire conditions. Which it did.


## The thousand-day gap is an analytical gap, not a regulatory one


Here’s what I need people to take from this, because it’s going to be read as a story about the town stonewalling, and that’s not the most important part.

Suffolk County Water Authority tests its wells. It tested these wells. It ran the standard PFAS panel, EPA Method 537.1, the one every utility in the country runs, the one most home test kits run, the one I’ve been writing about since September. That method looks for a defined list of compounds. PFPrA, three carbons long, is not on the list. There was no standard method for it in a drinking water matrix. There was no drinking water standard to compare it against. So for nearly three years, the wellfield was sampled, the results came back, and the thing that was in the water wasn’t on the report.

Nobody did anything wrong by the rules. The rules didn’t have a line for this. That’s the failure mode: not a cover-up, not negligence, a method that answered the question it was asked and was never asked the right one.

The county’s plume investigation can be “mostly complete” and it will have mapped a contaminant that was invisible to the standard tool for a thousand days. Every private well owner in the flow path who tested in 2024 with a standard panel and got a clean report has a piece of paper that was true and useless.


## Why “below screening level” and “no biomonitoring” don’t add up


The town’s position, as reported, rests on private well results coming back below the screening level. Two problems.

First, below which screening level, by which method? If the private wells were tested by 537.1, the result for PFPrA is not “below screening.” It’s “not measured.” Those are different answers and only one of them is reassuring.

Second, a screening level is a decision tool for whether to investigate further. It is not a statement that the people who drank the water for three years took no dose. Biomonitoring, a blood PFAS panel, is the only way to answer that question for an individual. It’s not expensive. The state Health Department has run blood PFAS programs in other contaminated communities in New York. Hoosick Falls got one: the geometric mean PFOA in 1,573 people on the village water system was 42.1 ppb, 27 times the national average, per a 2025 paper by state Health Department authors. Newburgh got one too, and found PFOS and PFHxS elevated against national levels. Declining it here while the investigation is “mostly complete” tells residents that the question of what happened to them is closed before anyone asked their bodies.

I’m not going to speculate about why. There are liability reasons a town might decline, and there are budget reasons, and there’s the honest scientific objection that a blood PFPrA result has no reference range yet so nobody could tell a resident what their number means. That last one is real. It’s also an argument for collecting the samples now and interpreting them later, not for never collecting them.


## What a resident in the flow path should actually do


If you’re on a private well south of the plant or anywhere near the Bridgehampton Road wellfield: test with a method that sees short-chain compounds. Ask the lab, by name, whether PFPrA is on the analyte list and by what method. Direct-injection short-chain panels and total organofluorine methods like EPA 1621 are the right tools; 537.1 is not. If you tested before and got a clean report, find out what method was used before you trust it.

If you want to know your own exposure: ask your doctor for a serum PFAS panel. Commercial labs run them. The standard panel will show PFOS, which was also in the water, and that number does have reference ranges. For PFPrA you’ll be an early data point. That’s not nothing; it’s how reference ranges get built.

And if you’re in the room at the next town meeting, the question I’d ask isn’t “why won’t you biomonitor.” It’s “which method did you use on the private wells, and when did you start looking for PFPrA.” The answer to that is the whole story.


## What “mostly complete” should mean


An investigation of a plume that was invisible for a thousand days isn’t complete until it answers three questions the standard method couldn’t. How far has PFPrA actually traveled, measured with a method that sees it, not inferred from where PFOS is. What’s in the private wells in the flow path, by that same method, with the results given to the owners in writing. And what happens to the people who drank it, which is a health question the environmental agencies will say isn’t theirs and the health agency has said it has no plans for.

If the county’s report answers the first two and nobody answers the third, “mostly complete” is accurate. It’s the “mostly” that’ll be living next door to the wellfield for the next thirty years.


## The bigger thing


Grid-scale battery storage is being sited across Long Island and the Northeast. Every one of those facilities has a fire-suppression design, and most of them use the same chemistry. DEC’s finding that the suppressant breaks down into short-chain PFAS under real fire conditions is the finding that should change those designs, and the siting reviews, and the groundwater monitoring plans, before the next fire, not a thousand days after it.

## What we know

- Suffolk County told residents the Cove Hollow Road PFAS plume investigation is “mostly complete” (East Hampton Star, October 22, 2026).
- ~5,000 lbs Novec 1230 plus ~2 million gallons of sprinkler water were applied during the May 2023 fire; DEC says the mixture broke down into ultra-short-chain PFAS.
- PFPrA first detected ~1,000 days after the fire, with PFOS, both well above drinking water limits, in a wellfield ~2,600 feet away.
- NYS Health Department: “no plans” for an area-wide health study. Town has declined resident requests for biomonitoring.
- DEC’s October 14 source investigation update is the primary document.

## What we don’t know yet

- Which analytical method was used on private wells reported as “below screening level.”
- Full plume extent and the final responsible-party determination.
- Whether any resident has had serum PFAS testing.
