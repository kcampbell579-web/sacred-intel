Researchers at Columbia’s Mailman School used matched samples from 396 participants in the Health Effects of Arsenic Longitudinal Study: raw rice, cooked rice, the water it was cooked in, and a spot urine sample. The question was which sample best reflects total arsenic exposure. Published in Environmental Research. The population is in Bangladesh, where arsenic in groundwater is a long-running public health disaster and rice is most of the diet.

The answer: cooked rice. Not raw rice, not the water. And the reason is the finding that travels.


## Rice is a sponge


Rice absorbs water as it cooks. If the water has arsenic in it, the rice absorbs the arsenic, and the amount it ends up with depends on both the rice and the water. The study found that cooking rice in water above 50 micrograms per liter could push nominally safe raw rice, rice that would pass a test on its own, above 200 nanograms of inorganic arsenic per gram dry weight after cooking. That’s the threshold the researchers used; it’s in the range of international guidance for rice.

So a household could test its rice, pass. Test its water, find arsenic. And conclude, reasonably, that the exposure is the water they drink, when the larger dose might be the rice they cook in it. Neither sample alone describes the meal. The cooked rice does, because it’s the thing that was actually eaten.

Across a wide range of water arsenic concentrations, cooked rice tracked urinary arsenic better than raw rice or water did. At low concentrations in both, neither explained much of the variation, which is honest and expected: when the exposure is small, other sources dominate.


## The method point


What you sample determines what exposure you find. That’s the whole paper, and it’s the thing I’d put on a slide for anyone who designs a sampling plan.

Water is the obvious matrix because it’s regulated and easy. It’s also the wrong matrix for an exposure that happens during food preparation. The same logic applies far beyond rice: lead in water that ends up in formula, nitrate in water that ends up in soup, PFAS in water that ends up in whatever you boil. The exposure is the end product, and the end product is rarely what gets tested.


## Why it matters on Long Island


Arsenic is naturally present in groundwater across the Northeast, from the geology, not from pollution. Long Island’s aquifer has it in places, usually at low levels but sometimes above the 10 microgram per liter federal limit in private wells, and private wells are the ones nobody’s required to test.

A homeowner on a well with arsenic at, say, 20 micrograms per liter is told the water’s over the limit and should be treated or not drunk. Most will switch to bottled for drinking and keep cooking with the tap. Pasta, rice, beans, soup. Rice especially, if it’s a staple in the house. The study says that’s where the dose may actually be, and nobody’s told them.

If your well has arsenic above the limit: treat the water or don’t cook with it either. If you’re a consultant: ask what the household cooks with before you decide what to sample. The lab report on the water answers the question you asked. The cooked rice answers the one that matters.

## What we know

- Columbia Mailman School study (Environmental Research, October 7, 2026): matched raw rice, cooked rice, cooking water, and spot urine from 396 HEALS participants in Bangladesh.
- Cooking rice in water above 50 µg/L arsenic can push nominally safe raw rice above 200 ng inorganic arsenic/g dry weight.
- Cooked rice was the more reliable dietary exposure indicator across a wide range of water arsenic; neither rice nor water explained much urinary variance when both were low.

## What we don’t know yet

- How the finding transfers to Western diets where rice is a smaller share of intake.
