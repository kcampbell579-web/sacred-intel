_Disclosure: I run a company that sells home testing kits, including for PFAS. Noted._

Most PFAS-in-pesticide stories are about contamination: PFAS leaching into the product from fluorinated plastic containers, or showing up as an impurity. This isn’t that. Some pesticides contain PFAS as an intentional active ingredient, because the fluorinated chemistry is what makes the molecule work. That category is largely unregulated at both the federal and state level, and it’s going to stay that way in California for now.

Inside Climate News reports that legislation to prohibit PFAS-containing pesticides was defeated after significant industry spending against it. I’ll leave the lobbying story to them; it’s their reporting and it’s detailed. What I care about is what the bill’s failure leaves in place.


## The pathway

Pesticides get applied to agricultural soil, to turf, and around houses for pest control. If the active ingredient is a PFAS, then every application puts a forever chemical directly into soil, where it doesn’t break down, where it moves with water, and where it eventually shows up in groundwater or in the vegetables. Turf treatments put it on the lawn kids play on. Residential pest treatments put it around the foundation.

Nobody’s sampling for this yet, because nobody’s been asked to. Standard PFAS panels look for the compounds that came out of foam and industrial discharge. A pesticide-derived PFAS might be a different molecule entirely, one that isn’t on the panel. So you could test a residential soil sample, get a clean result, and have PFAS in it anyway.

Why I expect this to show up in residential questions before it shows up in regulation

Because that’s the order it always goes. A homeowner tests their soil for one reason, gets a strange result, and asks why. A consultant pulls the application records for the property and finds a product name. Someone looks up the active ingredient. And then the question is on the table long before any agency has a rule for it.

If you do residential environmental work, know which pesticide active ingredients are fluorinated, and start asking about lawn and pest treatment history on intake forms. It’s one question. It’ll matter.


## What we know

- Inside Climate News (September 9, 2026) reports California legislation to ban PFAS-containing pesticides was defeated following industry lobbying.
- PFAS as an intentional pesticide ingredient remains largely unregulated federally and at the state level.


## What we don’t know yet

- Which specific active ingredients are affected and their use volumes in New York.
- Whether standard analytical PFAS panels detect pesticide-derived PFAS compounds.
