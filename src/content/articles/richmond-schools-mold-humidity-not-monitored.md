Richmond Public Schools briefed its board on mold prevention after Obama and Bellevue elementary schools were closed temporarily at the start of the year. Both got deep cleaning and air quality testing before reopening, with periodic retesting planned. Then the district’s chief operations officer, Patrick Herrell, said the thing that should be in every facilities manager’s ear: many of the district’s older HVAC systems can be monitored remotely for temperature but not for humidity. “Humidity is the biggest risk we face.” Obama Elementary can’t be monitored for humidity at all. Replacement sensors are on order.

So the district measures and controls temperature, and the variable that actually drives mold goes unmeasured. That’s not a Richmond problem. It’s the standard condition of most school buildings, office buildings, and houses in the country.


## Why humidity, not temperature


Mold needs three things: spores, which are everywhere; a food source, which is any organic surface, drywall paper, ceiling tile, dust; and moisture. Temperature matters only at the extremes. The control variable is water, and in a building without a leak, water means humidity.

Above about 60 percent relative humidity, sustained, surfaces start to hold enough moisture for growth. Above 70, it’s reliable. A building that runs cold and damp, which is what happens when an air conditioning system is oversized or short-cycles, cools the air without pulling the moisture out, and the relative humidity climbs even as the thermostat reads a comfortable 72. A school that’s closed for the summer with the AC on a setback schedule is a humidity chamber for ten weeks. That’s the start-of-year closure pattern, and it’s why the closures happen in September.

A temperature sensor can’t see any of this. The building looks fine on the dashboard right up until someone smells it.


## What the air test after cleanup tells you


Both schools got air quality testing before reopening. That’s a clearance test: spore counts inside compared to outside, after remediation, to show the cleanup worked. It’s the right thing to do and it’s what I’d do.

Here’s what it doesn’t do. It tells you the air was acceptable on the day of the test. If the condition that grew the mold, 70 percent humidity in a closed building, hasn’t changed, the test is a snapshot of the one day between the cleanup and the regrowth. A clearance test after remediation means little if the moisture control is unchanged, and in this case the district has said, on the record, that it can’t measure the moisture.

The sensors on order are the actual fix. A humidity sensor in every mechanical zone, logged continuously, with an alarm at 60 percent, costs less than one air test and prevents the next closure instead of certifying the last one.


## For anyone who manages a building, or a house


Measure humidity. A $15 hygrometer in the basement and one in the worst room. If it reads over 60 for days at a time, you have a moisture problem whether or not you can see mold yet. Fix the moisture: dehumidifier, ventilation, AC that runs long enough to dry the air. The mold test is for after, to prove the fix worked. The hygrometer is for before, so you don’t need the fix.
