About 200 industrial facilities, roughly half of the 400 or so that applied, have received presidential exemptions from nine Clean Air Act rules, according to an Environmental Defense Fund analysis reported by Harvest Public Media. Seventy facilities across 17 states got two-year exemptions from limits on hazardous air pollutants including chloroprene and ethylene oxide, about half of them on the Gulf Coast. Texas has 28 exempted facilities, the most of any state. EPA had estimated the ethylene oxide and chloroprene rule would cut the population at elevated cancer risk near those plants by 96 percent. EDF and the Sierra Club are suing.

I wrote about the HON rule and its fenceline monitoring in September. This is the exemption mechanism catching up with it.


## What an exemption is


The Clean Air Act has a provision allowing the President to exempt a stationary source from a hazardous air pollutant standard for up to two years, renewable, if the technology to meet the standard isn’t available and it’s in the national security interest. It’s been used rarely. It’s being used now at scale, and the courts will decide whether the statutory conditions were met. I’m not going to predict that.

What I can tell you is what the exemption covers, which is narrower than the headline.


## What it doesn’t do


It doesn’t change state rules. Many states have their own air toxics programs with their own limits. Texas has one. Louisiana has one. A federal exemption doesn’t exempt a facility from a state permit condition, and a state agency that wants to enforce still can.

It doesn’t change OSHA. Ethylene oxide has a permissible exposure limit of 1 part per million as an eight-hour average, and an action level of 0.5. Workers inside the exempted plants are covered by that regardless of what comes out the stack. The exemption is about the neighbors, not the employees.

It doesn’t change tort exposure. A company that emits a known carcinogen at levels a federal rule would have prohibited, with an exemption in hand, is still a company whose neighbors can sue for the harm. The exemption may be a defense; it’s not an immunity. And a plaintiff’s expert with fenceline data is going to use the rule’s own risk estimate, that 96 percent figure, as the measure of what the exemption cost.

It doesn’t change the chemistry. Ethylene oxide is a carcinogen at low concentrations. The exemption changes the legal requirement to control it. It doesn’t change what it does in a lung.


## Where the burden goes


When source control relaxes, the question “what am I breathing” doesn’t go away. It moves from the regulator to the fenceline. Community monitoring, perimeter sampling, the passive samplers I wrote about with the HON rule: those become the only data, and designing them so they hold up becomes the work.

For an industrial client with an exemption: it’s a two-year federal pass on one rule. Check the state permit, keep the OSHA program running, and understand that the data the neighbors collect over the next two years is the data a jury will see.

## What we know

- ~200 facilities (of ~400 applicants) received presidential exemptions from nine Clean Air Act rules, per EDF analysis (KCUR / Harvest Public Media, October 7, 2026).
- 70 facilities in 17 states received two-year exemptions from hazardous air pollutant limits including chloroprene and ethylene oxide; ~half on the Gulf Coast; Texas has 28.
- EPA had estimated the rule would cut the population at elevated cancer risk near those plants by 96%. EDF and Sierra Club are suing.

## What we don’t know yet

- The full facility list and the stated basis for each exemption.
- Litigation outcome.
