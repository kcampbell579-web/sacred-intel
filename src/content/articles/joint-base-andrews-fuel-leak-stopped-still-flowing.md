Joint Base Andrews said it stopped a fuel leak. Water testing keeps showing jet-fuel-related compounds flushing into Piscataway Creek anyway, especially after heavy rain. Maryland officials are pressing the base for an explanation.

There’s no contradiction here, and the reason there isn’t is the point.

“The leak is stopped” is a statement about the source. A valve was closed, a pipe was fixed, a tank was emptied. It can be completely true.

“Fuel is still reaching the creek” is a statement about transport. Fuel that leaked before the fix is in the soil and the shallow groundwater. It doesn’t know the valve closed. It keeps moving downgradient, toward the creek, for as long as it takes to flush, which for a petroleum release in a saturated zone can be months to years. Every rain pushes more of it.

So the base can be right about the source and Maryland can be right about the creek, and the only way to reconcile them is the thing Maryland is doing: sampling after the “fix,” repeatedly, at the receptor, instead of accepting a closure statement.


## Why this matters for anyone relying on a remediation endpoint


A closure letter says the responsible party’s work is done. A monitoring record says whether the contamination is. They’re issued by different people for different reasons, and the first one is often signed before the second one is clean.

For a client with a release on their property, or downgradient of someone else’s: the question isn’t whether the leak was stopped. It’s whether the plume has stopped moving, and the only evidence for that is a trend line from repeated sampling at the edge. One clean round means the plume wasn’t there that day. Four declining rounds over a year mean it’s attenuating. A responsible party that offers the first as proof of the second is offering a photograph as a movie.
