The City of Sheyenne, North Dakota collected five drinking water samples between June 1 and September 30. Two exceeded the federal lead action level of 15 parts per billion. The city’s notice attributes the lead to premise plumbing, the pipes and fixtures inside homes, rather than to the distribution mains. It says not every property is affected, warns that infants, children, and pregnant residents face the greatest risk, and does not disclose the measured concentrations.

Five samples is how a small system complies with the Lead and Copper Rule. The rule sets sample counts by population; a very small system can meet its obligation with five homes. Two of five over the action level is 40 percent, which triggers the system to act. It tells the individual homeowner in Sheyenne nothing about their own tap, because their tap wasn’t one of the five.


## Read the notice as an instruction


The city says the lead comes from premise plumbing. That’s probably right; small towns rarely have lead mains, and old brass fixtures and lead solder in houses built before 1986 are the usual source. But look at what that means. The utility is saying: the water we send you is fine, the lead gets in after it enters your house, and we can’t tell you whether yours does.

That’s the case for testing your own tap, made by the water system itself. The only measurement that describes a specific house is a sample from that house, first draw in the morning, and a flushed sample after, to separate the fixture from the main. The utility’s five samples are a compliance exercise. Yours is an answer.

For the residents who got this notice and want to know their number: that’s a $30 test, and it’s the one the city is telling you to take.
